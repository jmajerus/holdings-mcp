## 1. The Natural Thirst to Understand

In `terminology-mcp`'s `human-work` concept scheme, **curiosity** (`urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:curiosity`) is defined as *"a state in which you want to learn more about something."* Yet curiosity is never merely technical acquisition; it begins as a relational posture toward reality. Writing in *Fidelity*, Thich Nhat Hanh frames that impulse as a two-way desire both to understand and to be understood:

<!-- quote 829 concepts="human-work:curiosity" -->
<div class="iasp-quote" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" data-quote-id="829" data-citekey="hanh2007fidelity" data-work-key="zotero:users/827407/9I4PBQ68" data-quote-locator="chap. 4" about="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:curiosity" data-concepts="human-work:curiosity" data-concept-uris="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:curiosity" data-concept-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/curiosity">

> EACH OF US HAS A DEEP DESIRE TO know and understand the world and to be known and understood in return. That is a deep natural thirst. But often this thirst causes us to wait for something outside of ourselves. (Hanh 2007, chap. 4)[^hanh-note]

</div>


<div class="note insight" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" typeof="Concept" resource="urn:iasp:concept:insight" data-iasp-delivery="note" data-iasp-modifiers="insight" data-iasp-concepts="urn:iasp:concept:note urn:iasp:concept:insight" data-iasp-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/iasp/slugs/note https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/iasp/slugs/insight" about="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:curiosity" data-concepts="human-work:curiosity" data-concept-uris="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:curiosity" data-concept-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/curiosity" style="background-color: #f0f4fa; border-left: 4px solid #5b7fa6; padding: 14px 18px; margin: 18px 0; border-radius: 0 6px 6px 0; font-family: Georgia, 'Times New Roman', serif; line-height: 1.5;">

## 2. Systems Leadership and the Discipline of Curiosity

In complex technical and organizational systems, curiosity has to move from a passive thirst to an active discipline. In *Learning Systems Thinking*, Diana Montalion observes that while technical fields naturally attract curious minds, systems leadership requires directing that curiosity toward how wholes interact rather than isolated parts:

<!-- quote 828 concepts="human-work:curiosity human-work:learning" -->
<div class="iasp-quote" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" data-quote-id="828" data-citekey="montalion2024learning" data-work-key="zotero:users/827407/9U7ZWKJ6" data-quote-locator="p. 211" about="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:curiosity urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:learning" data-concepts="human-work:curiosity human-work:learning" data-concept-uris="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:curiosity urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:learning" data-concept-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/curiosity https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/learning">

> “I am only passionately curious.” This describes many of the people I’ve worked with in tech. We are curious about different things, but generally speaking, the industry attracts curious people. (Montalion 2024, 211)[^montalion-note]

## 3. When the Brain Works in Reverse

Why does a faculty that Conrad called *"the most respectable faculty of the human mind"* so often stall inside organizations and public life? In *Suspicious Minds*, Rob Brotherton describes the cognitive mechanism that quietly inverts inquiry from discovery into self-confirmation:

<!-- quote 831 concepts="human-work:confirmation-bias" -->
<div class="iasp-quote" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" data-quote-id="831" data-citekey="brotherton2015suspicious" data-work-key="zotero:users/827407/J2U9WJQC" data-quote-locator="chap. 11" about="urn:terminology-mcp:term:t_60fab99204f4479680df679ef2d94434" data-concepts="human-work:confirmation-bias" data-concept-uris="urn:terminology-mcp:term:t_60fab99204f4479680df679ef2d94434" data-concept-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/confirmation-bias">

> We like to think our beliefs are founded on a fair survey of the best available evidence; first you find out the facts, then you come to a rational conclusion. But the reality is that our brains often work in reverse. The conclusion comes first, then our brain seeks out and shapes the evidence to fit what it already believes. And it does all this behind the scenes, leaving us with the illusion that we’ve diligently reviewed the evidence and arrived at the only reasonable conclusion. This tendency is called confirmation bias, and it colors our thinking in three distinct ways. (Brotherton 2015, chap. 11)[^brotherton-note]

</div>


<div class="note contrast" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" typeof="Concept" resource="urn:iasp:concept:contrast" data-iasp-delivery="note" data-iasp-modifiers="contrast" data-iasp-concepts="urn:iasp:concept:note urn:iasp:concept:contrast" data-iasp-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/iasp/slugs/note https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/iasp/slugs/contrast" about="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:curiosity urn:terminology-mcp:term:t_60fab99204f4479680df679ef2d94434" data-concepts="human-work:curiosity human-work:confirmation-bias" data-concept-uris="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:curiosity urn:terminology-mcp:term:t_60fab99204f4479680df679ef2d94434" data-concept-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/curiosity https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/confirmation-bias" style="background-color: #fff3e0; border-left: 4px solid #f57c00; padding: 14px 18px; margin: 18px 0; border-radius: 0 6px 6px 0; font-family: Georgia, 'Times New Roman', serif; line-height: 1.5;">
<p style="margin: 0 0 4px 0; font-size: 1.1em;">⇆</p>

**Open inquiry vs. reverse-engineered certainty:** Where systems thinking (`montalion2024learning`) treats anomalies as invitations to revise our mental model, confirmation bias (`brotherton2015suspicious`) treats anomalies as material to be explained away so the prior conclusion survives intact.

</div>

An authoring workflow—especially one assisted by AI—faces the exact same temptation. If an agent paraphrases from memory or cherry-picks unverified snippets, it builds a smooth chamber of confirmation bias. Anchoring every quotation to a verified corpus text (`quote-mcp` + `corpus-mcp`) and making rhetorical intent explicit (`iasp-mcp`) keeps the text accountable to its sources.
